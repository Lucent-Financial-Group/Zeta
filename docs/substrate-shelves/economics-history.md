## Part I — Economics shelf

### 1 — Classical political economy

**Adam Smith** — _The Wealth of Nations_ (1776).
Division of labour, specialisation, the price mechanism, and the invisible hand as a
coordination device. The founding text of market economics. B-0046 names Smith directly.

**David Ricardo** — _On the Principles of Political Economy and Taxation_ (1817).
Comparative advantage, rent theory, and the distribution of income among wages, profit,
and rent. Ricardo operationalised Smithian insights into a formal model.

**Karl Marx** — _Capital_ (Vol. I 1867; Vols. II–III posthumous).
Labour theory of value, surplus value, accumulation, and systemic crisis. Regardless of
normative verdict, Marx is the most rigorous early analyst of how time/energy (labour-time)
flows through industrial substrate. B-0046 names Marx directly.

**John Stuart Mill** — _Principles of Political Economy_ (1848).
The bridge between Ricardian classicism and later welfare economics. Mill introduced the
distinction between production laws (natural) and distribution laws (social/institutional).

---

### 2 — Austrian school and the knowledge problem

**Ludwig von Mises** — _Human Action_ (1949); _The Theory of Money and Credit_ (1912).
Praxeology: the study of purposeful human action as the foundation of economics. The
socialist-calculation debate: dispersed knowledge cannot be centralised without information
loss. B-0046 names Mises directly.

**Friedrich Hayek** — _The Use of Knowledge in Society_ (1945, AER); _Prices and Production_
(1931); _The Pure Theory of Capital_ (1941).
Prices as distributed information processors. Spontaneous order vs. constructed order.
The knowledge problem as a substrate-distributed-computation claim — composes with the
factory's retraction-native operator algebra (each price revision is an update propagation).
B-0046 names Hayek directly.

**Israel Kirzner** — _Competition and Entrepreneurship_ (1973).
Entrepreneurial alertness as the discovery mechanism for unexploited arbitrage. The
Austrian micro-dynamic complement to Hayek's macro-information theory.

---

### 3 — Keynesian and post-Keynesian

**John Maynard Keynes** — _The General Theory of Employment, Interest and Money_ (1936);
_A Tract on Monetary Reform_ (1923).
Aggregate demand, liquidity preference, animal spirits, and the non-neutrality of money in
the short run. Keynes introduced the idea that macroeconomic equilibria can be stable at
below-full-employment levels. B-0046 names Keynes directly.

**Hyman Minsky** — _Stabilizing an Unstable Economy_ (1986); _Can "It" Happen Again?_ (1982).
The financial instability hypothesis: stability breeds instability. Minsky cycles map
directly onto substrate decay — Hedge → Speculative → Ponzi financing are a three-phase
Higgs-decay pattern. Stage-2 resonance candidate.

**John Kenneth Galbraith** — _The Affluent Society_ (1958); _The New Industrial State_ (1967).
Countervailing power and the role of large institutions in shaping demand. Galbraith's
"revised sequence" (corporations shape consumer preferences rather than serve them) is a
reflexivity precursor.

---

### 4 — Monetarism and quantity theory

**Milton Friedman** — _A Monetary History of the United States_ (1963, with Schwartz);
_The Optimum Quantity of Money_ (1969).
Money supply as the primary determinant of nominal income and the price level. The
Great Depression as a monetary contraction, not a market failure. B-0046 names Friedman
directly.

**Paul Samuelson** — _Economics_ (1948, textbook); _Foundations of Economic Analysis_ (1947).
Neoclassical synthesis: Keynesian demand management + Walrasian general equilibrium.
Samuelson's revealed preference and envelope theorem gave microeconomics its formal language.
B-0046 names Samuelson directly.

---

### 5 — Institutional and evolutionary economics

**Thorstein Veblen** — _The Theory of the Leisure Class_ (1899).
Conspicuous consumption, pecuniary emulation, and the ceremonial-vs-instrumental dichotomy.
Veblen's critique: standard economics treats institutions as given; they are the subject
matter.

**Karl Polanyi** — _The Great Transformation_ (1944).
The embeddedness thesis: markets are not natural; they are constructed and embedded in
social relations. The double movement: market expansion → protective counter-movement.
B-0046 names Polanyi directly.

**Douglass North** — _Institutions, Institutional Change and Economic Performance_ (1990).
Institutions as the rules of the game; transaction costs as the friction they reduce or
impose. North's framework treats institutional evolution as an incremental path-dependent
process — analogous to the factory's retraction-native history log.

---

### 6 — Heterodox and debt history

**David Graeber** — _Debt: The First 5,000 Years_ (2011).
Debt precedes markets and barter by millennia. Money emerges from debt obligations, not
commodity exchange. Graeber challenges standard economic anthropology at its foundation.
B-0046 names Graeber directly (debt history register).

**Michael Hudson** — _Super Imperialism_ (1972); _…and forgive them their debts_ (2018);
_The Destiny of Civilization_ (2022).
Finance capitalism as a rent-extraction substrate. The jubilee tradition as a retraction-log
mechanism — periodic debt cancellation as a systemic debt-bomb defuse. Composes with
Stage-4 history-as-retraction-log scope.

---

### 7 — Complexity, reflexivity, and systems

**George Soros** — _The Alchemy of Finance_ (1987); _The Crash of 2008_ (2008).
Reflexivity: market participants' beliefs affect the fundamentals they believe they are
predicting. A self-referential feedback loop rather than an equilibrium system. B-0046
names Soros directly.

**W. Brian Arthur** — _The Economy as an Evolving Complex System_ (1988); _Increasing Returns
and Path Dependence in the Economy_ (1994).
Increasing returns, lock-in, and path dependence. The economy as a complex adaptive system.
Connects to Kauffman's autocatalytic sets (biology shelf Stage 1).

**Eric Beinhocker** — _The Origin of Wealth_ (2006).
Evolution as the core process of economic change. Business plans as memes; companies as
evolutionary algorithms. Synthesises complexity economics into a comprehensive framework.

---

### 8 — Distribution and inequality

**Thomas Piketty** — _Capital in the Twenty-First Century_ (2013); _Capital and Ideology_ (2019).
r > g as the long-run inequality driver. Piketty's data corpus is the most comprehensive
empirical time-series for wealth distribution ever assembled. B-0046 names Piketty directly.

**Anthony Atkinson** — _Inequality: What Can Be Done?_ (2015).
The policy counterpart to Piketty: specific mechanisms and interventions. Atkinson's
inequality measures (Atkinson index) are standard.

---

### 9 — Commons, governance, and plural economics

**Elinor Ostrom** — _Governing the Commons_ (1990); _Understanding Institutional Diversity_ (2005).
Nobel Prize 2009. Commons are not doomed to tragedy; communities develop institutional
arrangements that sustain shared resources. The eight design principles for robust
commons governance. B-0046 names Ostrom directly. High Stage-2 resonance candidate
for the harmonious-division pole.

**Kate Raworth** — _Doughnut Economics_ (2017).
The doughnut model: a social foundation floor + an ecological ceiling. Economics within
planetary boundaries. Plural-plural framing.

## Part II — History shelf

### 1 — Longue durée and civilisational scale

**Fernand Braudel** — _The Mediterranean and the Mediterranean World in the Age of Philip II_
(1949, 2 vols.); _Civilization and Capitalism, 15th–18th Century_ (3 vols., 1979–1984).
Longue durée: the slow-moving structures (geography, climate, material life) that constrain
and shape historical events. Braudel's annales framework treats history as a multi-timescale
phenomenon — composes directly with the factory's temporal-substrate modeling. B-0046 names
Braudel directly.

**Immanuel Wallerstein** — _The Modern World-System_ (Vol. I 1974; Vol. IV 2011).
World-systems theory: core, semi-periphery, periphery as a structural account of global
capitalism since the 16th century. The world-system as the unit of analysis, not the
nation-state.

**Karl Jaspers** — _The Origin and Goal of History_ (1949).
The Axial Age (800–200 BCE): simultaneous emergence of philosophy, monotheism, and
rational cosmology across Greece, Persia, India, and China. A substrate-convergence event
at civilisational scale.

---

### 2 — Collapse and complexity

**Joseph Tainter** — _The Collapse of Complex Societies_ (1988).
Collapse as a rapid simplification: societies invest in complexity to solve problems;
marginal returns on complexity decline; collapse is the adaptive response when the
investment stops paying. Direct empirical defense-surface for the yin-yang invariant
(over-unification → bomb-pole collapse). B-0046 names Tainter directly; deferred to
Stage-4 for formal retraction-log treatment.

**Jared Diamond** — _Guns, Germs, and Steel_ (1997); _Collapse: How Societies Choose to Fail
or Succeed_ (2005).
_Guns, Germs, and Steel_: geographic and environmental determinants of Eurasian advantage.
_Collapse_: five-factor model of societal collapse (environmental damage, climate change,
hostile neighbors, friendly trade partners, political/cultural response). B-0046 names
Diamond directly; deferred to Stage 4 as empirical defense-surface.

**Peter Turchin** — _Ages of Discord_ (2016); _Secular Cycles_ (2009, with Nefedov).
Cliodynamics: mathematical modeling of historical dynamics. Elite overproduction and
popular immiseration as the two-pulse collapse driver. Secular cycles (integration →
stagnation → disintegration → recovery) as a quantitative retraction-log.

---

### 3 — Technology and material history

**Joel Mokyr** — _The Lever of Riches_ (1990); _The Gifts of Athena_ (2002).
Technological change as the engine of the Industrial Revolution. Propositional vs.
prescriptive knowledge: knowing-that vs. knowing-how. The Enlightenment as a change in
epistemic regime, not just political ideas.

**Robert Gordon** — _The Rise and Fall of American Growth_ (2016).
The singular nature of 1870–1970 innovation: electricity, internal combustion, running
water, modern communications. Post-1970 slowdown as a return to historical norm, not
a blip. Caution for techno-optimism framing.

**Vaclav Smil** — _Energy and Civilization: A History_ (2017); _Growth_ (2019).
Energy as the master variable of civilisation. Every major historical transition is an
energy-regime transition. Smil denominates history in joules — the most direct
implementation of B-0046's "time/energy not money" framing. High Stage-2 resonance
candidate.

---

### 4 — State, legibility, and control

**James C. Scott** — _Seeing Like a State_ (1998); _Against the Grain_ (2017); _The Art of
Not Being Governed_ (2009).
High-modernist failures: state simplification schemes that destroy local knowledge.
_Against the Grain_: early states as grain-tax-capture mechanisms; "barbarians" as
state-evaders, not primitives. B-0046 names Scott directly.

**Francis Fukuyama** — _The Origins of Political Order_ (2011); _Political Order and Political
Decay_ (2014).
State-building, rule of law, and democratic accountability as three independent variables
that must all be present for stable governance. Political decay as institutional
repatrimonialization. Composes with Tainter's complexity-collapse framework.

**Charles Tilly** — _Coercion, Capital, and European States_ (1990).
War-making as state-making: European states emerged from competition between coercion
specialists (warriors) and capital specialists (merchants). The state as a protection
racket that became institutionalized.

---

### 5 — Great divergence and convergence

**Kenneth Pomeranz** — _The Great Divergence: China, Europe, and the Making of the Modern
World Economy_ (2000).
Why did the Industrial Revolution happen in Britain rather than China? Contingent factors
(coal geography, Atlantic trade) rather than cultural or institutional superiority.
B-0046 names Pomeranz directly.

**Eric Jones** — _The European Miracle_ (1981).
The European fragmented-state-system as an incubator for institutional innovation.
Competition between states prevented any single power from monopolizing and stagnating.
A political-fragmentation-as-diversity argument.

**Andre Gunder Frank** — _ReOrient: Global Economy in the Asian Age_ (1998).
Asia-centric reframe: Asia (especially China) was the center of the world economy until
1800; European dominance is a brief aberration, not a permanent condition.

---

### 6 — Anthropology of exchange and value

**Marcel Mauss** — _The Gift_ (1925).
Gift exchange as the foundational social bond; reciprocity as the precondition for
community. The gift economy predates and persists alongside market exchange.

**Marshall Sahlins** — _Stone Age Economics_ (1972).
Hunter-gatherers as the "original affluent society": needs low + satisfactions high.
The Zen road to affluence. Challenges the scarcity premise of standard economics.

**Bronisław Malinowski** — _Argonauts of the Western Pacific_ (1922).
Kula ring: ceremonial exchange of arm-shells and necklaces among Trobriand Islanders.
The first systematic anthropological account of non-market exchange networks.

---

### 7 — Intellectual history of economics

**Robert Heilbroner** — _The Worldly Philosophers_ (1953).
Intellectual biography of the great economists from Smith to Keynes and beyond. The
standard entry point for history-of-economic-thought. Stage-1 register: survey.

**Philip Mirowski** — _More Heat Than Light_ (1989); _Machine Dreams_ (2002);
_Never Let a Serious Crisis Go to Waste_ (2013).
Economics and physics: neoclassical economics was reverse-engineered from 19th-century
energy physics. The Mont Pelerin Society and the construction of neoliberal thought.
Critical science-studies perspective.

**Deirdre McCloskey** — _The Bourgeois Virtues_ (2006); _Bourgeois Dignity_ (2010);
_Bourgeois Equality_ (2016).
Rhetoric of economics: economic arguments are narrative and moral, not purely technical.
The Great Enrichment (1800–present, ~3000% real income growth) as the result of changed
attitudes toward commerce, not capital accumulation or institutions per se.

---

### 8 — War, empire, and geopolitics

**Paul Kennedy** — _The Rise and Fall of the Great Powers_ (1987).
Imperial overstretch: great powers decline when military commitments outgrow the economic
base. A quantitative study of hegemonic transitions from 1500 to the present.

**Niall Ferguson** — _The Ascent of Money_ (2008); _Civilization_ (2011).
Financial history as civilisational history. The six "killer apps" of Western civilisation.
Contested framing; included as a probe.

**Yuval Noah Harari** — _Sapiens: A Brief History of Humankind_ (2011); _Homo Deus_ (2015).
Cognitive revolution, agricultural revolution, and scientific revolution as the three
ruptures that made Homo sapiens dominant. Intersubjective fictions (money, gods, nations)
as the coordination substrate. B-0046 names Harari directly.

---

### 9 — Environmental and ecological history

**William McNeill** — _The Rise of the West_ (1963); _Plagues and Peoples_ (1976);
_The Pursuit of Power_ (1982).
Plagues as historical actors; the co-evolution of human populations and microorganisms.
Military-technological change and the rise of industrialism. B-0046 names McNeill directly.

**Alfred Crosby** — _The Columbian Exchange_ (1972); _Ecological Imperialism_ (1986).
Biological factors in historical conquest: European expansion succeeded partly because of
accompanying organisms (diseases, domesticated animals, weeds). A materialist ecological
account of the Great Divergence.

**John R. McNeill** — _Something New Under the Sun: An Environmental History of the
Twentieth-Century World_ (2000).
The 20th century as the most environmentally disruptive in human history. Energy throughput
and biosphere impact denominated in measurable physical units.
